Topic 1.1

Cell Structure

Cells are the basic unit of all living organisms. All cells share some features, but there are key differences between cell types you must know for the exam.

Eukaryotic cell A type of cell found in plants and animals that contains a nucleus. The genetic material (DNA) is enclosed within a nucleus. Animal cells, plant cells and algal cells are all eukaryotic. Size: usually 10–100 µm.
Prokaryotic cell A type of cell found in bacteria that does not contain a nucleus. The genetic material is not enclosed in a nucleus — it is found as a single loop of DNA free in the cytoplasm. Prokaryotic cells are much smaller, usually around 1–10 µm. All bacteria are prokaryotes.
Plasmid Small extra rings (loops) of DNA found in the cytoplasm of prokaryotic cells. They are separate from the main chromosomal DNA and may carry additional genes (e.g. antibiotic resistance). Not all bacteria have plasmids.
Organelle A specialised structure found inside a cell, e.g. nucleus, mitochondria, chloroplasts. Prokaryotic cells have no membrane-bound organelles at all.

Topic 1.2

Microscopy

Microscopes allow us to observe cells and sub-cellular structures that are too small to see with the naked eye. The development of electron microscopes greatly increased our understanding of cell biology.

Magnification How many times bigger an image appears compared to the actual size of the object. magnification = image size ÷ real object size
Resolution The ability to distinguish two different points in a specimen as separate from one another. Higher resolution means more detail is visible. Electron microscopes have better resolution than light microscopes because electrons have a shorter wavelength than visible light.
💡 Light Microscope
  • magnification up to ×1,500–2,000
  • resolution ~200 nm (0.2 µm)
  • specimens can view living or dead cells
  • cost cheap — widely available in schools
  • image colour; stains can be added
  • can see cells, nucleus, chloroplasts, cell wall
⚡ Electron Microscope
  • magnification up to ×2,000,000
  • resolution ~0.1–0.2 nm
  • specimens must be dead — high vacuum conditions
  • cost very expensive — specialist labs only
  • image black & white (false colour added later)
  • can see ribosomes, membranes, viruses, DNA
Why does this matter? The development of electron microscopes allowed scientists to observe sub-cellular structures like ribosomes for the first time. This is an example of how scientific knowledge develops as new technology becomes available.

Topic 1.3

Cell Division

Cells divide to produce new cells for growth, repair and replacement. You need to understand the cell cycle, mitosis, differentiation and specialised cells.

Chromosome Thread-like structures made of DNA found in the nucleus of eukaryotic cells. Human body cells contain 46 chromosomes (23 pairs). They carry genetic information in the form of genes.
Gene A small section of DNA that codes for the production of a specific protein. Different sequences of DNA bases code for different proteins, which determine the characteristics of an organism.
Mitosis A type of cell division in which one cell divides to produce two genetically identical daughter cells, each with the same number of chromosomes as the parent cell. Used for growth, repair and replacement of cells, and asexual reproduction.
Differentiation The process by which a cell changes to become specialised for its function. Most animal cells differentiate at an early stage in development. Most plant cells can differentiate throughout their entire life. When a cell differentiates, it produces the specific proteins required for that function.
Specialised cell A cell that is adapted to perform a specific function. Its structure is directly related to its function. Specialisation arises from the process of differentiation.

Differentiation in animals
Most animal cells differentiate at an early stage of development. Once differentiated, most cells can no longer divide to form other cell types. This is why damaged tissues (e.g. spinal cord) cannot repair themselves easily.
Differentiation in plants
Most plant cells retain the ability to differentiate throughout their life. Meristem cells in root tips and shoot tips remain undifferentiated permanently and continue to divide and differentiate as the plant grows.
Topic 1.4

Stem Cells & Cancer

Stem cells are undifferentiated cells with the potential to become many different specialised cell types. Their medical uses are a major area of scientific research — and ethical debate.

Stem cell An undifferentiated cell that is capable of dividing (by mitosis) to produce daughter cells that can either remain as stem cells or differentiate to become a specialised cell type.
Embryonic stem cells Found in early human embryos. They are pluripotent — they can differentiate into almost any type of cell in the body.
Adult stem cells Found in certain adult tissues, for example bone marrow. They are multipotent — they can only differentiate into a limited range of related cell types (e.g. red blood cells, white blood cells, platelets).
Meristematic cells (plant stem cells) Found in meristems at root tips and shoot tips. They are totipotent — they can develop into any type of plant cell throughout the plant's life. They can be used in tissue culture to produce clones of plants.
Therapeutic cloning A technique in which an embryo is produced with the same genetic information as the patient. Stem cells extracted from this embryo could be used to produce cells, tissues or organs to treat the patient. Because the genetic material matches, the transplanted tissue is less likely to be rejected by the immune system.
✓ Potential benefits
  • Treat type 1 diabetes — stem cells could replace the insulin-producing β-cells in the pancreas that have been destroyed by the immune system
  • Treat paralysis — stem cells could replace damaged nerve cells in the spinal cord
  • Therapeutic cloning produces a genetic match, reducing the risk of rejection
  • Plant meristematic cells used to grow clones in tissue culture — producing identical, disease-resistant plants
✗ Risks & ethical issues
  • Using embryonic stem cells requires destroying a human embryo — raises the question of when life begins
  • Some religious groups are opposed — they consider an embryo to already be a human life
  • Risk of transferred cells developing into tumours
  • Possible transmission of viral infections from donor cells
  • Adult stem cells are less versatile and more difficult to isolate

Cancer

Cancer The result of changes in cells that lead to uncontrolled cell division, forming a mass of cells called a tumour. Cancer is caused by mutations in the genes that control the cell cycle. These mutations can be inherited or caused by environmental factors.

Topic 1.5

Transport in Cells

Substances move into and out of cells by three processes. The AQA specification requires you to compare and contrast all three — this is one of the most common exam topics.

Diffusion The spreading out of particles from an area of higher concentration to an area of lower concentration. Diffusion is a passive process — it does not require energy from respiration. Net movement continues down the concentration gradient until particles are evenly spread (equilibrium).
Osmosis The diffusion of water molecules through a partially permeable membrane from a region of higher water concentration (dilute solution) to a region of lower water concentration (concentrated solution). Osmosis is a passive process — it does not require energy from respiration.
Active transport The movement of substances from a more dilute solution to a more concentrated solution — against the concentration gradient. Active transport requires energy from respiration and uses carrier proteins embedded in the cell membrane.

Required Practical 3 — Effect of osmosis on plant tissue:
Cut equal-sized cylinders of potato → pat dry → weigh each one → place each in a different concentration of salt or sugar solution → leave for the same time → remove, pat dry, reweigh → calculate % change in mass.

% change in mass = [(final mass − initial mass) ÷ initial mass] × 100

Results: In a dilute solution — water enters by osmosis → mass increases (positive %). In a concentrated solution — water leaves by osmosis → mass decreases (negative %). Where the line crosses zero = the concentration equals the solute concentration inside the potato cells.
